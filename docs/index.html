A multi-dataset benchmark of pretrained audio embeddings, supervised and unsupervised detectors, and a tiny int8 neural network designed for practical edge deployment.

What we wanted to answer

Can UAVs be detected reliably from sound, which representations work best across different datasets, can unsupervised methods compete, and how much performance can we retain in a model small enough for an embedded device?

Research design

Offline quality reference

We benchmarked frozen AST, YAMNet and Wav2Vec 2.0 embeddings, with and without triplet-loss adaptation, then trained supervised logistic regression and unsupervised OCSVM / Isolation Forest detectors on top.

Deployable edge track

A separate raw-waveform mini-SE-Net was trained, quantized to full int8 TFLite and measured for model size and CPU inference latency. It serves as the practical TinyML candidate rather than the maximum-accuracy reference.

Six datasets, including real field recordings

Three public datasets were paired with three datasets recorded by the team in real outdoor conditions. The field recordings were the hardest despite being much larger, showing that acoustic diversity matters more than raw sample count.

Why this matters

An unsupervised detector can be useful when UAV labels are scarce. On Electric UAVs, FPV and UAV, the best unsupervised AUC is only 0.009–0.012 below the best supervised probe.

But representation matters

Raw Wav2Vec 2.0 features were poorly aligned with rotor acoustics in several unsupervised runs. Strong audio representations were more important than simply choosing a sophisticated anomaly detector.

Triplet fine-tuning: useful, but not everywhere

Triplet adaptation did not universally improve the embeddings. Its value depended strongly on the dataset.

Where it helped

Electric UAVs and UAV improved in 8 of 9 matched combinations, with mean AUC gains of about +0.15 and +0.17. In the cross-dataset experiment, all 9 triplet variants improved.

Where it hurt

On the cleaner public datasets it was often unnecessary or harmful: it degraded 7 of 9 combinations on Binary Drone Audio and all 9 on Multiclass Drone Audio.

Most important triplet result

On FPV, AST-triplet lifted Isolation Forest from 0.483 to 0.911 AUC and OCSVM from 0.554 to 0.895. Adaptation was especially valuable for label-free detection on the team’s own recordings.

The edge-deployable model

The int8 mini-SE-Net takes the raw 16,000-sample waveform directly. It has about 7.4k parameters, is roughly 22.6 KB, and ran in about 1.5–2.5 ms per sample using the TFLite interpreter on a Colab host CPU.

The good news

On the curated public corpora, the tiny model stayed close to the much heavier embedding pipeline: 0.989 AUC on Binary Drone Audio and 0.975 on Multiclass Drone Audio.

The limitation

The field datasets exposed a larger accuracy gap, and latency was measured on a Colab CPU rather than the final target microcontroller. Hardware benchmarking is still required.

What the completed project shows

01

AST / YAMNet embeddings are strong references

Linear probes on pretrained embeddings dominated the benchmark, with best AUC values from 0.759 to 1.000.

02

Unsupervised UAV detection is genuinely viable

With the right embedding, OCSVM and Isolation Forest stayed close to supervised performance on most datasets.

03

Real-world data is much harder

The largest datasets were not the easiest. Recording diversity and domain shift mattered more than simply having more clips.

04

Triplet adaptation is domain-dependent

It helped most on field recordings and cross-dataset transfer, but was often unnecessary on curated public datasets.

05

TinyML is feasible

A ~22.6 KB int8 model reached up to 0.989 AUC, showing that meaningful UAV detection can fit into a very small footprint.

06

The next step is real hardware

The remaining engineering task is validating latency and accuracy on the actual embedded target and closing the field-data performance gap.
